Is the Planet Warming?

Temperature records for fourteen places, 1895–2025. One question, four stages: state your position, explore, argue the other side, then find out what holds.

About the data

Each row is one reading: a place, a year, and the average temperature over that whole year, in degrees Fahrenheit. There are 1,834 readings covering 1895 to 2025, published by NOAA's National Centers for Environmental Information: the six US places come from the U.S. Climate Divisional Database, the six continents and two polar regions from Climate at a Glance Global. There is nothing in this file about greenhouse gases; that question has a tool of its own.

The same quantity appears for fourteen places:

ColumnMeaning
year, decadeThe year of the reading, and the decade it falls in
locationSix places in the United States — Onslow County · North Carolina · California · Kansas · New Mexico · Contiguous U.S. — six continents — North America · South America · Europe · Africa · Asia · Oceania — and the two polar regions, Arctic and Antarctic. Onslow County is one county in North Carolina. The list runs from a single county up to whole continents, so the area being averaged grows by a factor of millions from one end to the other.
temp_fAverage temperature over the year, in °F. Blank for the continents and the poles — NOAA publishes those only as departures, not as absolute temperatures, so any fit against temp_f uses the 786 US rows alone
anomaly_fThe reading measured against that place's own 1901–2000 average. Onslow's average over that century was 61.59 °F, New Mexico's was 52.86 °F and the nation's was 52.01 °F, so this column lets places with different climates be put on one axis. This is the only column that covers all fourteen places, and the one to use when comparing them. The continent and polar values were published in °C and converted here by multiplying by 1.8; the baseline period is the same 1901–2000 either way.

These are area averages built from thousands of weather stations, adjusted for station moves and instrument changes and interpolated onto a grid. They are not the raw readings from any single thermometer. The continent series are land only, so they leave out the oceans that cover most of the planet. The two polar series deserve more caution than the rest: there were very few instruments at either pole before the late 1950s, so their early values lean heavily on interpolation between distant stations. For the Antarctic, NOAA's land and land-and-ocean series are identical figure for figure, which tells you the region as drawn here carries no separate ocean measurement at all.

What each region covers

NOAA builds the continents and poles from the 5° × 5° grid cells whose centre points fall in each region, then weights them by area. It does not publish the boundary polygons, so the descriptions below are each region's conventional extent rather than an exact specification. A 5° cell is roughly 550 km across, which is coarse enough to matter at a coastline or a border.

These regions overlap, so they cannot be added up or treated as independent. Onslow County sits inside North Carolina, which sits inside the contiguous U.S., which sits inside North America. The Arctic covers land that is also counted in North America, Europe and Asia. Fourteen rows for one year are not fourteen separate measurements of that year, and a result that holds in all fourteen is not fourteen independent confirmations of it.

A note on the filters

The Filters panel sits above every view. The year range there decides which part of the record everything else is computed from, so narrowing it changes every chart and every statistic on the page.

A note on the calculator

The Calculator makes new columns from existing ones, for all 523 readings at once. meanBy is the useful one here: meanBy("temp_f", "location", "decade") gives each reading the average for its own place and decade.

Reading a slope

If you work out a rate of change, the usual unit for temperature records is degrees Fahrenheit per decade. A slope of +0.5 °F per decade means the line rises half a degree every ten years, which would be about 5 °F per century if it held.

What you can use in a formula
  • Any column name, such as temp_f or year
  • Arithmetic: + - * / ( ) and powers with **
  • Comparisons give 1 (true) or 0 (false): location == "Onslow County", year > 1975
  • Choices: condition ? a : b, for example year < 1960 ? "early" : "late"
  • Functions: log(x) (natural), log10(x), sqrt(x), abs(x), round(x)
  • mean("temp_f"): the mean of a column over all rows
  • meanBy("temp_f", "location"): for each row, the mean of temp_f over all rows that share its location. You can list more than one column: meanBy("temp_f", "location", "decade")
